- Start from the person on the other side and what they cannot do today, in their words. Write the problem as someone experiences it, then the key insight, for example: nobody can say where a reported figure comes from, or which systems hold it, without reading the pipeline code. A problem statement with no person and no moment is a solution in disguise.
- Keep four things apart: the person, the problem and how often it occurs, the proposal the author of the ticket picked, and the need behind the request. When the ticket is only a proposal ("add a retry button"), recover the problem first.
- When the ticket asks to patch what people see, trace the cause before you scope the patch. The cause before the cure: decide the fix of the cause and record it when it still delivers what the ticket asked for; it goes into the session's one batch when it changes what the ticket delivers (the ticket would be retitled), is far larger than the value, or carries a risk. A patch scoped before the cause is known is paid for twice: a rate limit put in front of a slow path does not make it fast, gets removed, and the repeated cost is fixed in the end anyway.
- Tie every piece of scope to a value (time saved, an error that stops, something made possible, a cost that goes down, a risk or a compliance gap removed), an output (what exists and can be observed when it is done) and a measure (how anyone would know, with a number and a unit).
- When no value can be named, say so, with the cheaper alternative: "nothing new is needed" is a legitimate answer. A value you cannot name is a finding, not a gap to fill with guesses. When the cost is far above the value, recommend less or nothing, and say what the person gives up.
- When something only looks missing (a setting "for completeness", an edit path beside a read path), keep it out until the gap is confirmed real against what the person actually asked, and name it under what is out. Defaulting to "add the missing thing" causes scope creep and over-engineering.
- Hold every scope to the bar of a good take-home design assignment: think from the perspective of the person who uses the result, document the design decisions, the trade-off reasoning and any assumptions, and describe how it behaves in different scenarios. A scope that covers only the path where everything works is unfinished.
