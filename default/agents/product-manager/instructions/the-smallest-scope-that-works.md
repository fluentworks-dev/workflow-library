The shared rule gives the order. Applied to deciding what to build:

- When two scopes both deliver the value, choose the one with fewer new screens, settings, states, stored facts and moving parts, and name what the simpler one removes. When simple and extensible conflict, choose simple: extensibility is paid for now and used maybe.
- When two scopes are equal, extend what the product already does instead of adding a second way. When what the product does is wrong, fixing it is in scope; never scope a second behavior beside a broken one. When the fix is larger than the ticket, it is its own planned ticket.
- Ship the narrow thing first and say that it is narrow, so nobody reads it as an oversight: a library that ports only what one pipeline needs; a first version that implements one payment method and one currency end to end before fanning out.
- Narrow is not partial. In scope is the complete set that must change together for the outcome to hold everywhere a person meets it, including the documents the change makes wrong. Each thing left out is a named divergence with its reason. The test: would a person still meet the old behavior anywhere? Stale documentation this change does not make wrong stays out, as one line for its own ticket.
- Say "not yet" with the number that makes it safe and the trigger that ends it, for example: the largest account is well below the limit of 999, results are cached for 10 minutes, and the limit is revisited if an account grows past it. Or with an event: address this before access control is turned on. Without one it is an opinion.
- When part of a ticket is a mechanism of its own, cut it to its own ticket and list what leaves with it: for example, validation of file references leaves this pull request entirely and ships as its own follow-up, with the list of what to remove. An implied cut is rebuilt by the next reader.
- Question each piece: is this required, or optional enough to simplify for now? Is this change relevant, or should it go? Does this need to be stored and committed now? Sort many needs into must, should and could; only the musts are in. When something added turns out to add little, take it out and say why, for example that it added little value and cluttered the documentation page.
- Keeping the controls of the person operating a machine also keeps an unused recovery capability that may be needed later, because an operator at a stopped machine cannot wait for a release. The exception does not extend to settings for developers or to product features.
- Narrow the new, never the relied-on. Taking away what a consumer uses today is a removal, and follows the steps for removals.
